# Decision Memo: Habit Tracker for Climbers

## Verdict: DROP

**Score: 28/100** | Confidence: medium

> **A test subject, not a recommendation.** Chosen to exercise the consumer lane end to end: App Store mapping, review mining, ASO scoring, creator fit, freemium conversion, D30 retention. Nothing in your profile points at consumer fitness apps.

> Confidence is medium: App Store ratings and review counts were not obtainable, and no climbing app publishes conversion or retention data.

---

## Why This Score

Two findings decide it. The arithmetic first: freemium is compulsory because the direct competitor gives its core product away, and $4.99 at 2.5% conversion values an install at fifty cents against a cheapest channel costing $1.50. Doubling conversion does not fix it.

More interesting, the premise is unsupported. Across three competitors' documented complaints, climbers ask for force-gauge integration, periodization, in-workout grade logging and shorter sessions. Nobody complains about failing to keep habits.

## Top 3 Strengths

1. **Demand** (42/100): Both parent markets grow. Over 6 million US indoor climbing participants, gym count up 4.6% year on year, and habit tracking compounding at 14.2% toward $6.41 billion by 2034.
2. **Retention** (42/100): A real external trigger exists in the training session, and a streak that survives a deload week removes the usual reason climbers abandon generic habit apps.
3. **Competition** (32/100): One genuine gap remains. Nothing today joins habit tracking to climbing load, so nothing can say "your fingers have had three hard days, rest tomorrow".

## Top 3 Risks

1. **Monetization** (22/100): Crimpd, built by Lattice Training, publishes 100+ structured workouts free and charges $4.99 for premium. *If ignored:* you price against free from the brand climbers trust, give away your only differentiator to be visible, and convert at the bottom of a low band.
2. **Distribution** (26/100): The k-factor is 0.064. A streak chart is not shareable, so climbing's strong creator ecosystem is unreachable, and $4.99 funds no commission. *If ignored:* you build for an audience you have no way to reach at a cost you can absorb.
3. **Founder-market fit** (30/100): No climbing domain, no audience in the sport, no consumer app experience. *If ignored:* you compete on credibility against a coaching institution, in a community that weights practitioner status heavily.

## Riskiest Assumption

> "Climbers experience habit inconsistency as a problem they would pay to solve, rather than as a background fact of amateur training."

**Test it before building.** Fourteen days, under $30. Post in two or three climbing training communities asking a genuine question: what most gets in the way of your training, and what did you try? Do not mention habits, consistency or an app. Count unprompted mentions of consistency against not knowing what to train, plateauing, time and injury. In parallel, run a landing page for the finger-load guard to about 100 targeted visitors. **Pass: 30% of responses name consistency unprompted, and the page converts at 10% or better.**

## Pre-mortem: If This Fails in 12 Months

1. **Free was good enough.** Every prospective user compared it with Crimpd, found 100+ workouts at zero from a brand they trust, and never returned.
2. **The habit premise was wrong.** Downloads came from curiosity, D30 sat under 8%, and exit feedback said the app tracked something users did not think needed tracking.
3. **Acquisition never paid back.** ASO delivered installs at $1.50 against a fifty-cent install value, and there was no budget to test anything else.

## Devil's Advocate

1. **The saturation score describes climbing apps, not this product.** Nobody has built a training-load-aware habit tracker, and that is sometimes opportunity rather than proof of no demand.
2. **The conversion estimate does enormous work.** At 2.5% nothing survives; at a top-quartile 8% the monetization score changes materially.
3. **Paid-only works on paper.** Without a free tier, LTV reaches about $19.96 and ASO clears 13:1. That model was rejected on judgement, not arithmetic.

Objections two and three both require winning a pricing argument against a free product from the sport's coaching authority. Objection one offers no affordable way to reach climbers even if the gap is real.

## What To Do Now

**Drop it, and keep the method.** The competitor complaint set contradicted the problem statement, and it cost two searches to find out. On your next idea, mine complaints before writing the problem statement.

**Kill criteria:** already met. Monetization fell below the killer-dimension threshold, a structural failure rather than an execution one.

## If That Doesn't Work

The better-evidenced product here is a generator of short, adaptive climbing sessions. LatticePlan's sharpest complaint is that it prescribes 90-minute to 2.5-hour workouts that do not fit around a job, a pain climbers state in their own words.
